Credit risk is the risk of a loss because a borrower does not pay, or because the market comes to believe that the borrower is less likely to pay. Block A measures the expected loss on a single borrower. Block B asks what market prices say about default risk. Block C covers the losses beyond default: a downgrade, and a bad year for the whole portfolio.

Block A: One Borrower

A loan has a limited upside and a large downside: the lender receives the promised interest at best and can lose the whole amount at worst. The loss on one borrower depends on three questions. Will the borrower default? How much will the borrower owe at that moment? How much of it will the lender get back?

Key Concepts

Probability of default (PD): the probability that the borrower defaults within a given period, usually one year.

Exposure at default (EAD): the amount owed when the default happens. Borrowers in trouble draw on their credit lines, so EAD includes a part of the undrawn line. This part is the credit conversion factor (CCF). The Basel III standardised approach uses 40% for committed lines and 10% for lines the bank can cancel at any time.

Recovery rate: the share of the exposure the lender gets back after a default. It depends on collateral and seniority. Average recovery on US corporate bonds, 1983–2021 (Moody’s): first-lien 55%, senior unsecured 38%, junior subordinated 22%. The market convention of 40% comes from the senior unsecured figure.

Loss given default (LGD): 1 − recovery rate.

Theory: Expected Loss

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{EAD} &= \text{Drawn} + \text{CCF} \times (\text{Committed Line} - \text{Drawn}) \\ \text{LGD} &= 1 - \text{Recovery Rate} \\ \text{EL} &= \text{PD} \times \text{LGD} \times \text{EAD} \end{aligned} \]

  • EL = expected loss over the horizon of the PD (here one year)
  • EL in % of EAD is simply PD × LGD

Each component has its own lever: borrower selection lowers PD, collateral and seniority lower LGD, limits and covenants on undrawn lines lower EAD.

Expected and Unexpected Loss

  • Expected loss (EL): the average loss per year. It is a cost of doing business, like wages. It is covered by the loan spread and by provisions.

  • Unexpected loss (UL): the amount by which the loss of a bad year exceeds the average. It is covered by capital. Activity 6 calculates it.

Key Takeaway: The largest loan is not the largest risk. Expected loss depends on the product of three numbers, and a small unsecured loan to a weak borrower can cost more than a large loan to a strong one.

Theory: Three Ways to State a Default Probability

  • Cumulative default probability \(Q(t)\): default at any time up to year \(t\). It can only rise with \(t\).

  • Unconditional default probability for year \(t\): default during year \(t\), seen from today. \[ Q(t) - Q(t-1) \]

  • Conditional default probability for year \(t\): default during year \(t\), given no earlier default. Also called the marginal default probability. \[ \frac{Q(t) - Q(t-1)}{1 - Q(t-1)} \]

  • Hazard rate \(h\): the conditional default probability per year for a very short period. With an average hazard rate \(\bar{h}\) between today and year \(t\): \[ Q(t) = 1 - e^{-\bar{h}\,t} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \bar{h} = -\frac{1}{t}\ln\bigl(1 - Q(t)\bigr) \]

The three measures contain the same information. The hazard rate is the practical one: it gives a default probability for any horizon, not only for whole years.

Key Takeaway: For a good borrower the conditional default probability rises with time: the borrower can only get worse. For a weak borrower it falls: the next year or two are critical, and those who survive have usually recovered. A constant hazard rate is therefore an approximation.

Where the Horizon Matters: IFRS 9

Under IFRS 9 a bank holds a provision equal to the 12-month expected loss for a performing loan (Stage 1). When the credit risk of the loan has increased significantly, the provision rises to the lifetime expected loss (Stage 2). The borrower has not defaulted, but the PD in the calculation changes from a one-year to a multi-year figure.

Block B: What the Market Knows

The table in Activity 2 is history: an average over forty years and over many companies. A rating changes rarely. Market prices change every day, and investors with money at stake set them. Two markets carry information about default risk: the debt market, through credit spreads, and the equity market, through share prices.

Theory: Credit Spreads and Default Probabilities

Credit Default Swap (CDS)

A CDS is insurance against the default of a company. The buyer of protection pays a premium every year, the CDS spread, as a percentage of the insured amount. If the company defaults, the seller pays the loss: (1 − recovery rate) × insured amount.

A CDS spread and the spread of the company’s bond over the risk-free rate price the same risk, so the two are normally close to each other.

The spread must compensate the investor for the expected loss per year:

\[ s \approx h \times (1 - R) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad h = \frac{s}{1 - R} \]

  • \(s\) = credit spread per year (as a decimal: 128 bp = 0.0128)
  • \(R\) = recovery rate
  • \(h\) = average hazard rate implied by the spread

The default probability over \(t\) years is then \(Q(t) = 1 - e^{-h\,t}\), as in Activity 2.

A spread prices the product of default probability and loss given default. The default probability can be extracted only after the recovery rate has been assumed.

Real-world (historical) Risk-neutral (market-implied)
Source Default statistics of rating agencies Credit spreads, CDS spreads
Contains Expected defaults Expected defaults plus a risk premium
Changes Slowly Every day
Used for Expected loss, provisions, capital, scenarios Pricing and valuation of bonds, loans and hedges

Why the market-implied probability is higher: defaults do not happen independently. They cluster in recessions, when investors can least afford losses, and this risk cannot be diversified away. Investors demand a premium for bearing it, as equity investors do. Corporate bonds are also less liquid than government bonds, which adds to the spread.

Key Takeaway: A credit spread is not a forecast of how many borrowers will default. It is the price of default risk, and it is the fastest signal available.

Theory: The Merton Model

At the repayment date the shareholders receive the assets minus the debt, or nothing:

\[ E_T = \max(V_T - D,\ 0) \]

This is the payoff of a call option. The equity of a company is a call option on its assets, with the debt as the strike price. The Black–Scholes–Merton formula gives:

\[ \begin{aligned} E_0 &= V_0\,N(d_1) - D\,e^{-rT}\,N(d_2) \\[4pt] d_1 &= \frac{\ln(V_0/D) + (r + \sigma_V^2/2)\,T}{\sigma_V \sqrt{T}} \qquad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma_V \sqrt{T} \\[4pt] \text{PD} &= N(-d_2) \end{aligned} \]

  • \(V_0\) = value of the assets today, \(D\) = debt due at time \(T\)
  • \(\sigma_V\) = volatility of the asset value, \(r\) = risk-free rate
  • \(N(\cdot)\) = cumulative standard normal distribution, NORMSDIST in Excel
  • \(d_2\) = distance to default: by how many standard deviations the assets exceed the debt

The value of the debt is what remains: \(V_0 - E_0\).

From the model to practice: the asset value and its volatility cannot be observed. The share price and its volatility can. Commercial models solve for the two unknowns from these two observations and update the default probability every day.

Key Takeaway: Default risk has two drivers: leverage and the volatility of the assets. Both show up in the equity market long before they show up in a rating.

Block C: Losses beyond Default

Blocks A and B dealt with one event, the default. A lender can lose in two further ways. The borrower’s credit quality can fall without a default, and the value of its bonds falls with it. And many borrowers can default in the same year, so that the loss of the portfolio is far above its average.

Theory: Migration Risk

A downgrade raises the spread that investors demand, and the price of the bond falls until its yield has risen by the same amount:

\[ \text{Yield} = \text{Risk-free Rate} + \text{Credit Spread} \qquad\qquad \frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -\,\text{Duration} \times \Delta\text{Spread} \]

The same change in spread costs a long bond several times more than a short one.

Fallen Angels

A fallen angel is a bond downgraded from investment grade (BBB− or better) to high yield (BB+ or worse). This step matters more than any other on the rating scale:

  • Many pension funds, insurers and investment-grade funds are not allowed to hold high-yield bonds and must sell
  • The bond leaves the investment-grade indices, so index funds sell as well
  • Banks can no longer count it as a liquid asset
  • All of them sell at the same time, into a much smaller high-yield market

The price therefore tends to fall by more than the change in rating alone would justify.

Trading book and banking book: A bond held at fair value shows the loss at once. A loan or bond held at amortised cost shows no price loss, but the downgrade can move it to Stage 2 under IFRS 9, and the provision rises from the 12-month to the lifetime expected loss.

Transition matrix: a table of the probabilities that a rating changes to each other rating within one year. Ratings are stable: about nine in ten investment-grade issuers keep their rating category for the year. The outcomes for the lender are lopsided. The possible gain from an upgrade is small; the possible loss from a downgrade or a default is large and has a low probability.

Key Takeaway: A bond can lose a tenth of its value without missing a payment. For the 99% worst case of a BBB bond the downgrade matters; for the 99.9% worst case only the default matters.

Case Study: Ford (March 2020)

In March 2020 the pandemic closed car plants and showrooms. Ford stopped production in North America on 19 March.

  • 25 March 2020: S&P cuts Ford from BBB− to BB+. Moody’s already rates it high yield
  • Size: $35.8 billion of Ford debt leaves the investment-grade bond index at the end of the month. Ford is the largest fallen angel on record at that time
  • Others: Kraft Heinz (February) and Occidental Petroleum (March) lose their investment-grade ratings in the same weeks
  • 9 April 2020: the Federal Reserve extends its corporate bond purchases to companies that were investment grade on 22 March and have been downgraded since. Ford qualifies
  • October 2023: S&P raises Ford back to BBB−

  • Why did the central bank step in?
  • A bank held Ford bonds in its liquidity buffer. What changed for the bank on 25 March?

Theory: Default Correlation and Credit VaR

If defaults were independent, a large portfolio would lose almost exactly its expected loss every year. They are not independent: the default rate of all rated companies was 0.15% in 1981 and 4.19% in 2009. Vasicek’s model captures this with a single factor, the state of the economy, that drives all borrowers. It gives the worst-case default rate (WCDR), the default rate that is exceeded only with probability \(1 - X\):

\[ \text{WCDR} = N\!\left(\frac{N^{-1}(\text{PD}) + \sqrt{\rho}\;N^{-1}(X)}{\sqrt{1-\rho}}\right) \]

  • PD = one-year probability of default of each loan
  • \(\rho\) = default correlation between the borrowers
  • \(X\) = confidence level (99.9% in the Basel rules)
  • \(N^{-1}(\cdot)\) = inverse of the standard normal distribution, NORMSINV in Excel

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Credit VaR} &= \text{WCDR} \times \text{LGD} \times \text{EAD} \\ \text{Unexpected Loss} &= \text{Credit VaR} - \text{Expected Loss} \end{aligned} \]

Expected loss Unexpected loss
What it is The loss of an average year The extra loss of a very bad year
Depends on PD, LGD, EAD PD, LGD, EAD and correlation
Covered by Loan spread and provisions Capital
Diversification Does not reduce it Reduces it, as far as correlation allows

The formula is the basis of the Basel capital requirement for credit risk under the internal ratings-based approach, with a correlation between 12% and 24% for loans to companies. Week 11 uses the same split between expected loss and capital to measure the profitability of a business (RAROC).

Key Takeaway: The loss distribution of a loan portfolio is skewed: many years with small losses, and a few years with very large ones. Correlation decides how large. It is also the number that is hardest to estimate and that rises in a crisis.

Key Takeaways

  • Expected loss: PD × LGD × EAD is the average loss. It is priced into the spread and covered by provisions. The largest loan is not necessarily the largest risk.

  • Default probabilities: A PD belongs to a horizon. Cumulative probabilities, conditional probabilities and hazard rates are three views of the same information.

  • Market signals: Credit spreads and share prices react within days; ratings react within months. Market-implied default probabilities are higher than historical ones because they include a risk premium.

  • Migration: A downgrade causes a loss without a default. The step from investment grade to high yield is the most damaging, because many investors must sell at once.

  • Unexpected loss: Capital covers the loss of a very bad year. Its size depends on how strongly defaults move together.
