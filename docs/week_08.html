Learning Outcomes:

  • Calculate the cost of trading from an order book and split it into spread cost (exogenous) and market impact (endogenous)
  • Calculate the cost of liquidation and the liquidity-adjusted VaR of a portfolio in normal and stressed markets
  • Determine how long a position takes to liquidate and how much market risk it carries while it is being sold
  • Calculate the Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) and the Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR) and interpret them under stress
  • Explain how leverage and margin calls turn a price fall into forced sales and a liquidity spiral

Liquidity risk has two forms. Market liquidity risk is the risk that you cannot sell an asset quickly at a price close to its market value. Funding liquidity risk is the risk that you cannot meet your payments when they fall due. Block A covers the first, Block B the second, and Block C shows how the two feed each other.

Block A: Market Liquidity Risk

A quoted price is valid only for a small trade. The order book shows what happens to a larger one: each price level offers a limited number of shares, so a large order “walks the book” to worse and worse prices.

Activity 1: Walking the Order Book

Sheet OrderBook. NordTech trades at a mid price of $50.00. The book shows six bid levels and six ask levels.

Task 1.1: Sell at market

You sell 12,000 shares at market. Calculate:

  • Total proceeds and the average execution price
  • The liquidity cost in $ and in % of the value at mid

Task 1.2: Decompose the cost

Split the liquidity cost into two parts:

  • Spread cost: the cost if the whole order were executed at the best bid
  • Market impact: the additional cost of walking down the book

Task 1.3: Cover a short position

Another desk is short 8,000 shares and must buy them back at market. Calculate its liquidity cost in $ and in %.

Task 1.4: Order size

Change the sell order to 1,000, 4,000 and 20,000 shares.

  • How does the cost in % change with the order size?
  • Which part of the cost stays the same and which part grows?

Theory: Exogenous and Endogenous Liquidity

Two Sources of Liquidity Cost

  • Exogenous liquidity (market-driven): conditions that exist regardless of your trade. The bid-ask spread is the same for every small order.

  • Endogenous liquidity (position-driven): the effect of your own order. The larger the order relative to the depth of the book, the further the price moves against you.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Liquidity Cost} &= \text{Spread Cost} + \text{Market Impact} \\ \text{Spread Cost} &= Q \times (\text{Mid} - \text{Best Bid}) \\ \text{Market Impact} &= Q \times (\text{Best Bid} - \text{Average Price}) \end{aligned} \]

  • \(Q\) = number of shares sold
  • Mid = average of the best bid and the best ask
  • Average Price = total proceeds divided by \(Q\)

For a purchase the logic is the same on the other side of the book: the buyer pays the best ask plus the impact of walking up the ask levels.

Key Takeaway: Small trades face the market as it is. Large trades change the market. The spread cost grows in proportion to the order; the market impact grows faster.

Short Squeeze: GameStop, January 2021

Short sellers had sold more GameStop shares than were available for trading. When retail investors pushed the price up, the short sellers faced growing losses and had to buy the shares back. Each purchase walked up the ask side of the book and raised the price for the next buyer. The share price rose from below $20 at the start of January to a high of $483 on 28 January. Task 1.3 is this problem in miniature.

Theory: Liquidity-Adjusted VaR

Standard VaR assumes that positions can be sold at mid-market prices. Liquidity-adjusted VaR adds the cost of unwinding the portfolio:

\[ \text{LVaR} = \text{VaR} + \sum_{i=1}^{n} \tfrac{1}{2}\, s_i\, \alpha_i \]

  • \(s_i\) = proportional bid-ask spread of position \(i\): (ask − bid) / mid
  • \(\alpha_i\) = value of position \(i\) at mid
  • The factor ½ appears because a seller loses only half the spread, from mid to bid

In a stressed market the spread is wider. If the spread has mean \(\mu_i\) and standard deviation \(\sigma_i\):

\[ \text{LVaR}_{\text{stressed}} = \text{VaR} + \sum_{i=1}^{n} \tfrac{1}{2}\,(\mu_i + \lambda \sigma_i)\,\alpha_i \]

  • \(\lambda\) = number of standard deviations for the chosen confidence level (2.326 for 99% if spreads are normally distributed)

Key Takeaway: A portfolio can have an acceptable VaR and an unacceptable LVaR. The formula covers the spread only, so it is a lower bound: for positions that are large relative to the market, the market impact from Activity 1 comes on top.

Task 3.3: Stress

In a stress, daily volume falls by 60%. Repeat Tasks 3.1 and 3.2.

  • Selling everything today removes the market risk. What does it cost? (Think of Activity 1.)
  • Under which circumstances would you still sell everything today?

Theory: The Liquidation Trade-off

  • Immediate liquidation removes market risk at once, but pays the full spread and the maximum market impact.
  • Gradual liquidation reduces market impact, but leaves the position exposed to price moves while it is being sold.

\[ n = \frac{\text{Position}}{\text{Daily Volume} \times \text{Participation Rate}} \quad \text{(rounded up)} \]

If the same amount is sold at the end of each day, the position shrinks step by step and the risk is lower than for a position held for all \(n\) days:

\[ \text{Risk} = V \times \sigma \times \sqrt{\frac{(n+1)(2n+1)}{6n}} \]

  • \(V\) = position value at mid
  • \(\sigma\) = daily volatility
  • The term under the root is the equivalent holding period in days. For large \(n\) it is close to \(n/3\)

Key Takeaway: Liquidity risk is non-linear and procyclical. The time needed to exit is short when you do not need to sell and long when you do. And the choice between fast and slow liquidation exists only for an investor who is not forced to sell (Block C).

Block B: Funding Liquidity Risk

A bank funds long-term, illiquid assets (loans) with short-term liabilities (deposits). Deposits can leave within days; loans cannot be called in. The bank therefore needs a buffer of liquid assets, and a funding structure that does not disappear in a crisis. After 2008, Basel III introduced one ratio for each of these two requirements.

Key Concepts

High-Quality Liquid Assets (HQLA): assets that can be turned into cash quickly and with little loss of value, even in a stress.

  • Level 1 (0% haircut): cash, central bank reserves, government bonds
  • Level 2A (15% haircut): covered bonds and corporate bonds rated AA− or better
  • Level 2B (25–50% haircut): lower-rated corporate bonds, some equities

Haircut: the reduction of market value to reflect a sale in stressed conditions. A $100 bond with a 15% haircut counts as $85.

Level 2 cap: Level 2 assets may form at most 40% of total HQLA. Equivalently, Level 2 may be at most two thirds of Level 1.

Run-off rate: the share of a liability assumed to be withdrawn within 30 days of stress. Stable retail deposits 5%, wholesale funding from other banks 100%.

Inflow cap: expected inflows may offset at most 75% of outflows.

Theory: LCR and NSFR

\[ \text{LCR} = \frac{\text{HQLA}}{\text{Outflows} - \min(\text{Inflows},\ 0.75 \times \text{Outflows})} \geq 100\% \]

  • HQLA = Level 1 + min(Level 2 after haircuts, 2/3 × Level 1)
  • Outflows = each liability × its run-off rate
  • Inflows = contractual receipts in the next 30 days × their inflow rate

\[ \text{NSFR} = \frac{\text{Available Stable Funding (ASF)}}{\text{Required Stable Funding (RSF)}} \geq 100\% \]

  • ASF = each funding source × its ASF factor: how much of it will still be there in one year
  • RSF = each asset × its RSF factor: how much of it must be funded with stable money
LCR NSFR
Horizon 30 days 1 year
Question Can the bank survive a run? Is the funding structure sound?
Numerator Liquid assets Stable funding
Punishes Runnable funding, illiquid buffer Long assets funded with short money

Key Takeaway: Banks rarely fail slowly from bad loans; they fail within days from funding runs. The LCR helps a bank survive the run. The NSFR makes the run less likely.

The Death Spiral
  1. Doubts about the bank → wholesale funding is not renewed
  2. The bank sells liquid assets → the HQLA buffer shrinks
  3. The bank sells less liquid assets at fire-sale prices → losses
  4. Losses confirm the doubts → retail deposits leave
  5. The bank fails

Funding stress forces asset sales at Block A prices. This is where funding liquidity and market liquidity meet.

Case Study: Silicon Valley Bank (March 2023)

Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) had about $175 billion of deposits. Close to 90% of them exceeded the deposit-insurance limit, and most belonged to technology companies that shared the same investors. SVB had invested the money largely in long-dated bonds bought when interest rates were low. By the end of 2022 the unrealised loss on its held-to-maturity bonds alone was more than $15 billion.

  • 8 March: SVB announces that it has sold $21 billion of securities at a loss of $1.8 billion and needs to raise $2.25 billion of new capital
  • 9 March: customers withdraw $42 billion, about a quarter of all deposits, in one day
  • 10 March: a further $100 billion of withdrawals is expected; regulators close the bank in the morning

SVB was not subject to the LCR. US rules introduced in 2019 exempted banks of its size.

Discussion:

  • The LCR assumes that 10% of less stable retail deposits and 40% of non-operational corporate deposits leave within 30 days. Compare this with SVB.
  • Which features of SVB’s deposits made the run so fast?
  • SVB held many government bonds, the best liquid asset. Why did selling them make the situation worse? (Hint: what happens to a loss that was unrealised?)
  • Would an LCR above 100% have saved SVB?

Block C: Leverage and Liquidity Spirals

In Activity 3 the investor could choose between selling fast and selling slowly. A leveraged investor often has no choice. The lender demands margin: equity of at least a fixed share of the position. When prices fall, the investor must add cash or sell, immediately.

Activity 6: Margin Call and Forced Sale

Task 6.2: Margin call

How much cash must the fund post to meet the margin requirement again?

Task 6.3: Forced sale

The fund has no spare cash. How much of the position must it sell, in $M and in % of the position?

Task 6.4: Wipe-out

What price fall wipes out the fund’s equity completely?

Task 6.5: The spiral

The fund’s sales move the market: every $1M sold pushes the price down by a further 0.1%. Follow the forced sales round by round in the table.

  • How much does the fund sell in total? How far does the price fall in total?
  • Raise the price impact to 0.2% and to 0.3%. What happens?
  • Change the margin requirement to 50% and to 12.5% (adjust the equity to match). What happens?

Theory: Leverage and Liquidity Spirals

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Leverage} &= \frac{\text{Assets}}{\text{Equity}} \\[4pt] \text{Margin Call} &= m \times \text{Assets} - \text{Equity} \\[4pt] \text{Forced Sale} &= \text{Assets} - \frac{\text{Equity}}{m} \end{aligned} \]

  • \(m\) = margin requirement (equity / assets); the maximum leverage is \(1/m\)
  • Assets and Equity are measured after the price fall
  • A price fall of \(1/\text{Leverage}\) wipes out the equity
The Liquidity Spiral
  1. Prices fall → the leveraged investor loses equity
  2. Margin call → the investor must sell
  3. The sale pushes prices down further (market impact)
  4. Lower prices → more losses and a new margin call

Market liquidity and funding liquidity reinforce each other. The spiral is stronger when leverage is high, when positions are large relative to the market, and when many investors hold the same position.

Procyclical margins: Lenders and clearing houses raise margin requirements when volatility rises. The demand for cash therefore increases exactly when cash is hardest to find. During the GameStop episode, the broker Robinhood was asked by its clearing house for about $3 billion of additional collateral in one morning and restricted customer purchases of the stock.

Liquidity wrong-way risk: The need for liquidity and the cost of liquidity rise together. Risk models that treat market risk and funding risk separately miss this.

Liquidity black holes: A market needs buyers and sellers. Liquidity disappears when everyone wants to trade in the same direction, because they hold similar positions, follow similar risk limits or face the same margin calls. Examples: the October 1987 crash, LTCM in 1998, the 2008 crisis.

Case Study: Archegos Capital Management (March 2021)

Archegos was the family office of the investor Bill Hwang. It held very large positions in a handful of stocks, among them ViacomCBS and Discovery.

  • Hidden leverage: Archegos did not buy the shares. It used total return swaps with several prime brokers: each bank bought the shares, Archegos received the gains and losses and posted margin
  • Size: according to the US regulator, its capital grew from about $1.5 billion in March 2020 to more than $36 billion in March 2021, with a total exposure of up to $160 billion
  • No one saw the whole position: swaps did not have to be disclosed, and each bank knew only its own part

The collapse:

  1. 22 March: ViacomCBS announces a share sale and its price starts to fall
  2. Margin calls arrive from all prime brokers at once; Archegos cannot meet them
  3. The prime brokers discuss selling the positions gradually and together, without agreement
  4. 26 March: banks sell the shares in large blocks; ViacomCBS falls 27% in one day
  5. The banks that sell last sell at the lowest prices

Losses of the prime brokers (over $10 billion in total):

Credit Suisse 5.5
Nomura 2.9
Morgan Stanley 0.9
UBS 0.8

Goldman Sachs and Deutsche Bank sold early and reported no significant loss.

Discussion:

  • Translate the case into Activity 6. Who is the fund, what is the margin requirement, and why was the price impact much larger than in the activity?
  • It is the evening of 25 March and you are the risk manager of one prime broker. Do you sell tomorrow morning, or wait for an agreement with the other banks?
  • Each bank’s VaR for its Archegos exposure looked acceptable. Which assumptions from Block A did it miss?

Key Takeaways

  • Market liquidity: The cost of trading has an exogenous part (the spread) and an endogenous part (market impact). The second grows faster than the order.

  • Liquidity-adjusted VaR: VaR values positions at mid. LVaR adds half the spread on every position, with a wider spread in stress.

  • Time: Large positions take days to sell and carry market risk while they are sold. In a stress the horizon becomes longer.

  • Funding liquidity: The LCR asks whether a bank survives 30 days of outflows; the NSFR asks whether long-term assets are funded with stable money. SVB shows how fast uninsured deposits can leave.

  • Spirals: Leverage turns a price fall into forced sales, and forced sales into further price falls. Liquidity is plentiful when nobody needs it and scarce when everybody does.
